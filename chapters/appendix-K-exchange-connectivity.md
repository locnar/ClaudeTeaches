# Appendix K — Exchange Connectivity & the Physical Layer

> **Consolidates the infrastructure layer** beneath the software: the NIC and its features (Ch. 75), PTP time distribution and hardware timestamping (Ch. 78), reliable-multicast feeds (Ch. 72), the datacenter fabric (Ch. 80), inline hardware paths (Ch. 91 FPGA, Ch. 92 SmartNIC), and the wire-to-wire measurement that closes the book (Ch. 120). Every latency budget in this book has a floor set by physics and cabling that no code can beat — this appendix names that floor: where the box sits, how the bytes get to the exchange, and how time is distributed at the venue.

**How to use this:** this is *orientation*, not a tuning guide — it explains the constraints the rest of the book optimizes *within*, and it is largely below the C++ layer. It's here so "colocation," "cross-connect," "microwave," and "grandmaster" are not black boxes when they appear in the performance chapters. Nothing here is legal, regulatory, or commercial advice; venue policies, telecom regulation, and vendor offerings change fast, so the specifics are illustrative of an era, not current quotes.

---

## K.1 Colocation: cages, cross-connects, power & cooling

**Colocation** ("colo") is renting rack space in — or immediately adjacent to — the exchange's own data center, so your matching-engine round-trip is measured in microseconds of cable, not milliseconds of metro fiber.

- The exchange operates a data center (e.g. the well-known equities venues in Mahwah, Carteret, and Secaucus, NJ; CME in Aurora, IL). Firms rent **cages** or **racks** there and connect to the exchange's network via a **cross-connect** — a physical cable from your rack to the exchange's access switch, ordered as a service from the facility.
- The latency significance is simply **"same building"**: light and electrons are fast, but distance is real (K.2), so being in the exchange's hall rather than a metro data center removes tens to hundreds of microseconds of one-way delay. This is the single biggest latency decision an HFT firm makes, and it dwarfs most software optimization — which is *why* the software must then be excellent to matter at all.
- Practical constraints: **power and cooling** budgets per rack (dense low-latency servers with high-clock CPUs and FPGAs run hot — Ch. 6), **space** limits, and the facility's **meet-me room** where carriers and the exchange interconnect. Access is often waitlisted and expensive; the scarcity is part of the competitive moat.

## K.2 The wire: fiber vs. copper, and latency-equalized cabling

Once colocated, the remaining intra-facility latency is dominated by cable length and medium.

- **Propagation delay** in optical fiber is roughly **5 ns per meter** (light travels at about two-thirds of *c* in glass, ~200,000 km/s). Copper (twinax/DAC) is comparable. So a 10 m difference in cross-connect length is ~50 ns — a meaningful fraction of a tick-to-trade budget (Ch. 120).
- Because that length difference is monetizable, exchanges commonly issue **length-equalized cross-connects**: every colo tenant, regardless of where their cage physically sits in the hall, gets a cable trimmed to the *same* length as the longest one, so no tenant has a cabling advantage over another. Fairness by construction. (Within your own rack, you still minimize patch lengths and connector count — every connector and meter is latency and a little jitter.)
- **Fiber vs. copper** at short range is a wash on latency; the choice is driven by transceiver cost, port density, and the NIC's supported media (Ch. 75). The interesting medium choice is *between* sites (K.3), not within the hall.

## K.3 Wireless: microwave and millimeter-wave

For *inter-city* links — e.g. carrying a price from Chicago's futures market to New Jersey's equities venues to trade a correlation — the medium matters enormously, because of a physics quirk:

- Light travels at ~**0.67 c** in glass fiber but at ~**0.99 c** in air. A **point-to-point microwave** (or **millimeter-wave**) link through the atmosphere is therefore ~50% faster per kilometer than fiber, *and* it can follow a straighter line-of-sight path than fiber that must route along rights-of-way. Over ~1200 km (Chicago–NJ), that combination saves multiple milliseconds — the difference between winning and losing a latency-arbitrage trade.
- The trade-offs: microwave has **far lower bandwidth** than fiber (so only the most latency-critical, compact signals go over it — often just top-of-book or a few symbols; the domain encodings that make them compact are Ch. 109 §109.4.8's, applied before the link instead of after a capture), and it is **weather-sensitive** (rain fade, atmospheric ducting), so firms run **hybrid** networks that fail over to fiber when the wireless link degrades. Networks are built from chains of relay **towers**, and the best routes are themselves scarce, contested assets.
- **Millimeter-wave** and even experimental free-space-optical / hollow-core-fiber links push further on the same idea: get closer to *c*, over a straighter path, for the critical bytes.

**What the radio path costs, and when it fails.** A microwave link is a chain of 20–40 km hops between towers, each hop a transmitter, a receiver and a *regeneration* (the signal is demodulated and re-modulated at every tower — a few µs each, which is why hop count, not just distance, sets the latency and why the best routes are the ones with the fewest, tallest towers on a clear line of sight with Fresnel-zone clearance). Capacity is tens to a few hundred Mb/s per channel (licensed 6–11 GHz bands; E-band millimeter-wave reaches Gb/s over shorter hops), against a feed that is Gb/s at the open — so *everything* that crosses it is filtered and **compacted**: a symbol subset, top-of-book deltas only, a custom binary encoding with delta-of-delta timestamps and varint prices (Ch. 109 §109.4.8's domain encodings, applied before the link; Ch. 85 §85.4.4's custom reliable UDP as the transport). The modem's **FEC and interleaving** are a latency-vs-availability knob: stronger coding and a longer interleaver survive deeper fades at the cost of tens of µs of block delay, so the lowest-latency setting is the one that fails first in rain. **Fade behaviour** is the operational fact: rain attenuation (worse at higher frequency — E-band fades in moderate rain, 6 GHz in storms), atmospheric ducting and multipath produce *bit-error bursts* that the modem's FEC first absorbs (latency unchanged), then loses (packet loss, which looks like a gap — Ch. 72), then the link drops (which looks like a dark feed — Ch. 74). The network operator's management plane reports the receive-signal level and the BER per hop; the trading system's **fade detector** reads them beside its own per-line loss and one-way-delay gauges (Ch. 73 §73.4.4, Ch. 115) and declares the microwave copy *degraded* before the loss reaches the book.

**Arbitrating heterogeneous-latency copies of the same feed.** A hybrid network delivers the same market data twice: the microwave copy milliseconds *earlier*, the fiber copy complete and late. This is Ch. 70 §70.4.3's A/B arbitration with the lines' latencies differing by milliseconds instead of microseconds, and three things change. The **reorder/arbitration window** is sized to the latency *difference* (ms, not µs — the fast copy's sequence numbers run thousands ahead of the slow copy's), so the fiber copy is almost never "first" and exists to fill gaps and to confirm. The **policy** is "fast copy drives, slow copy fills and signals": a gap on the microwave copy is filled from fiber only if the fill arrives inside the staleness budget (Ch. 50 §50.4.3) — otherwise the strategy is told it is trading on fiber-latency data and widens or pauses — and a sustained condition where fiber is first *is* the fade signal. And **compaction means the copies differ**: the microwave stream is a subset (symbols, levels) of the fiber stream, so the arbitration is per symbol and per field, and the book-building code must accept a top-of-book-only update from one copy and full depth from the other without treating the difference as a gap (Ch. 73 §73.2.1's "normalize the key, leave the rest venue-native" applies between the two copies of one venue).

The takeaway for a software engineer: the *medium* can dominate the entire tick-to-trade budget on a cross-venue strategy, and it is chosen (and paid for) at the infrastructure layer — your code lives inside whatever floor it sets; what the software owns is the compaction encoder, the fade detector, and the fast/slow arbitration policy.

## K.4 Exchange access: ports, gateways, entitlements & throttles

Getting bytes onto the exchange is a gated, metered, regulated interface — not an open socket.

- **Order-entry gateways and ports:** you connect to specific exchange **gateway** hosts through provisioned **ports** (sessions), each with an identity, capacity, and often a cost. Some venues offer premium low-latency ports or gateway placement.
- **Market-data entitlements:** you subscribe (and pay) for specific **feeds** — the depth-of-book multicast (J.4), top-of-book, trades, reference data — and are **entitled** only to what you've licensed. A feed handler (Ch. 70) only sees the streams the firm is entitled to.
- **Message-rate throttles and fair-access rules:** exchanges impose **rate limits** (orders/second per port) and enforce **fair-access** provisions so no participant can monopolize the gateway; exceed the throttle and messages are rejected or delayed. Your order-submission logic must respect these — the risk gate's TSC token buckets (Ch. 104 §104.4.3) enforce them per port, the order gateway (Ch. 103, 105) owns the session they apply to, and the pacing techniques of Ch. 79 shape the bursts — and the budget for doing so is part of the tick-to-trade decomposition (Ch. 120).
- **Sponsored / direct market access (DMA/SA)** and **pre-trade risk mandates:** regulators require pre-trade risk controls on the order path — in the US, **SEC Rule 15c3-5** (the "Market Access Rule") mandates checks (max order size, price collars, position limits) *before* an order reaches the exchange, whether the firm self-clears or accesses through a sponsor. These checks sit on the critical path (Ch. 120, J.3) and are non-negotiable latency you must budget for — you cannot simply optimize them away.

## K.5 Time distribution at the venue

Comparing timestamps across machines and firms — the basis of true wire-to-wire measurement (Ch. 78) and of regulatory reporting — requires a shared, disciplined time source.

- A **grandmaster clock** in the facility, disciplined by a **GNSS/GPS** reference (with a rooftop antenna), is the authoritative time source. It distributes time to hosts via **PTP** (Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588 — Ch. 78), which — with **hardware timestamping** in the NIC (Ch. 75) and on-path **boundary/transparent clocks** in the switches — achieves sub-microsecond, often sub-100-nanosecond, alignment across machines.
- **PPS** (Pulse-Per-Second) is the GNSS receiver's once-per-second edge (usually alongside a 10 MHz reference) carried over coax: it disciplines the **grandmaster** and any reference clock with a 1PPS input, and it is what you put on a scope to verify synchronization at the hardware level. A **boundary clock** (a PTP-aware switch) is disciplined by PTP messages from its upstream master and in turn serves its downstream ports; it does not take a PPS feed. **Oscillator quality** (OCXO or rubidium) and **holdover** — how far the clock drifts when GNSS is lost — are the grandmaster's engineering concerns (the topic Ch. 78 develops, and where White Rabbit pushes to sub-nanosecond).
- **Regulatory clock-sync requirements:** rules such as **MiFID II RTS 25** in Europe mandate documented maximum divergence from UTC (e.g. 100 µs for many HFT activities) and timestamp granularity — so accurate time distribution is both a *measurement* necessity (you cannot compute wire-to-wire latency across two boxes without it — Ch. 78, 120) and a *compliance* obligation. This is why the NIC-hardware-timestamp / PTP chain (Ch. 75–76) appears in a low-latency book at all.

## K.6 Redundancy & path diversity

Low latency is worthless if the link is down during the open, so the physical layer is built for resilience as well as speed:

- **A/B feed lines** (J.4, Ch. 72) are carried over **diverse physical paths** — separate fiber runs, separate switches — so a single cut or device failure loses at most one line, and the consumer's A/B arbitration (Ch. 72) rides through the gap.
- **Redundant cross-connects and gateways** give order entry a failover path; sessions are designed to reconnect and resynchronize (Ch. 106 — persisted sequence numbers, resends and gap fills; Ch. 112 — which gateway may hold the session after a failure) without losing order state.
- The trade-off is **latency vs. resilience**: the most diverse path is rarely the shortest, so firms balance a fast primary against a safe secondary — the same tension the process-topology and fault-domain discussion raises in software (Ch. 103).

## K.7 The trade-offs: latency, cost, and regulation

The physical layer is where the latency arms race meets economics and rules:

- **What a nanosecond costs:** colocation, equalized cross-connects, microwave bandwidth, GNSS-disciplined time, and FPGA-integrated NICs (Ch. 91, 92) are large, ongoing capital and operating costs. Each buys a diminishing slice of latency, so firms spend where the marginal nanosecond still changes fill rates — and *stop* where the physical floor makes further software optimization pointless (or vice versa: past a point, only the software is left to improve). How to *measure* that — the post-trade join of captures, order journal and venue timestamps into fill-rate-vs-latency and markout curves — is Ch. 120 §120.4.5.
- **Regulation shapes the field:** fair-access and equalized-cabling rules deliberately cap some advantages; market-access (15c3-5) and clock-sync (MiFID II RTS 25) rules add mandatory latency and infrastructure. The physical layer is not a pure free-for-all; it is an engineered, regulated environment.
- **The framing for Ch. 120:** the end-to-end tick-to-trade budget the book closes with is the sum of a *physical* floor (this appendix — colocation, cabling, the exchange link, the risk gate) and an *engineering* budget (everything in Parts I–XIII). Knowing which is which tells you whether your next nanosecond comes from a code change or a cross-connect order.

## K.8 Regulation that touches latency engineering

K.4 and K.7 name the rules as motivation ("mandatory latency"); this section is the requirements map — each rule decomposed into the code path it constrains, the measurable requirement it imposes and the chapter that satisfies it. It is jurisdiction-neutral where it can be and **dated (2026)** where it cannot: regulation moves faster than this book, so treat the table as the shape of the obligations and verify the current text with compliance before building to it.

| Rule (jurisdiction) | What it requires, as engineering | Code path | Measurable requirement | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SEC Rule 15c3-5** — Market Access (US) | pre-trade controls on *every* order before the venue: max order size/notional, price collars, credit/capital thresholds, duplicate and erroneous-order checks; the broker-dealer owns them even under sponsored access; direct and exclusive control; annual CEO certification | the risk gate on the order path | the gate runs on 100% of orders (a counter that must equal orders sent), its limits are the ones in the signed configuration, and its latency is budgeted (~40 ns measured) | Ch. 104 §104.2.1, §104.4.1; Appendix M.3 |
| **MiFID II RTS 6** — algorithmic trading controls (EU/UK) | **kill functionality** that cancels all unexecuted orders immediately and survives the algorithm's failure; pre-trade limits per instrument/trader/firm; **conformance testing** against each venue and **stress testing** of the algorithm (message rates, market conditions); an **algorithm identifier** on every order; real-time monitoring and alerts; an annual self-assessment with records | kill-switch path in a surviving process; the gate; the gateway's encode template; the telemetry/alerting tier; the test and release lifecycle | time from trigger to the venue's "zero resting" ack (measured, drilled); the algo id field present on 100% of orders (an encode-template invariant); the conformance session passed per venue per release; stress runs on record | Ch. 104 §104.4.5–§104.4.6, Ch. 107 §107.4.6, Ch. 103 §103.4.3 (the supervisor's kill), Ch. 106 §106.4.2 (the template field), Ch. 100 §100.4.4, Ch. 116 §116.4.7, Ch. 119 §119.4.6 |

| **MiFIR Art. 25 / RTS 24** — order record keeping (EU/UK) | every order event (receipt, transmission, modification, cancellation, execution) recorded with specified fields, timestamps and identifiers (client, trader, algo), retained ≥ 5 years, reproducible on request | the capture and order journal | the record carries the mandated fields; retention tier and integrity (CRC, signed manifests); a replay reconstructs any day | Ch. 109 §109.4.2, §109.4.4, §109.4.8; Ch. 38 §38.4.5 |
| **CAT (Consolidated Audit Trail)** — SEC Rule 613 / FINRA (US) | order events reported with timestamps at ≤ 1 ms (and finer where the firm's clock allows), linkage keys that tie a child order to its parent and route, clock synchronisation ≤ 50 ms to NIST (industry members; finer in practice), daily submission with error correction | the order journal's event schema; the clock chain | every reportable event has a CAT-shaped record with a linkage key; the clock offset is logged; the daily file reconciles against the journal | Ch. 109 §109.4.2, Ch. 105 §105.2.1 (identity and linkage), Ch. 78 |

| **MAR / market-abuse surveillance** (EU) and **FINRA Rule 3110** (US) | surveillance of the firm's own order flow for manipulative patterns, with records | off the hot path: the journal and post-trade analytics | the journal is complete (gap-free) and the surveillance job consumes it daily | Ch. 109 §109.4.4, Ch. 120 §120.4.5 |

Three engineering consequences that recur: **a surviving process must own the kill** (RTS 6's "immediately, regardless of the algorithm's state" is why Ch. 103 puts the supervisor on the purge port); **the record is the system of record** (RTS 24/CAT fields are the journal's schema, not an export — a field you did not capture cannot be reconstructed); and **the clock is a compliance component** (RTS 25's 1 µs and the gate's refusal to arm on a clock out of tolerance are the same check). Where a rule names a number — 100 µs, 1 µs, 50 ms, 5 years — the book's instrument for it is a *measured, logged* value, and the readiness gate (Appendix M) is where it is asserted before trading.

## K.9 Site redundancy and disaster recovery

K.6 is redundancy *within* a facility. Site loss — power, fibre cut at the building, a flood, the venue's own primary site failing — is handled one level up, and the physics decides the design:

- **Cross-site replication is asynchronous by construction.** Light in fibre is ~5 ns per metre: a second site 10 km away is 50 µs one-way, a metro pair 50–100 km is 0.25–0.5 ms, and a regional DR site hundreds of km is milliseconds — against a tick-to-trade budget of ~2 µs (Ch. 120 §120.3) and a sequencer hop of ~100–150 ns (Ch. 111 §111.3). No design in which the order path *waits* for a remote site survives the arithmetic, so every cross-site mechanism is **journal shipping**: the sequenced log (Ch. 111) streams to the remote site after the fact, the remote replicas fold it (Ch. 103 §103.2.2 — the same deterministic `apply`), and the remote state lags by the link's latency plus whatever the shipping batches. That lag *is* the RPO.
- **Tiers, with their numbers.** The vocabulary is **RPO** (recovery point objective — how much of the sequenced log the recovered site may be missing), **RTO** (recovery time objective — time from the loss to trading again) and **MTTR** (the measured mean, with its tail, over drills):

  | Tier | What runs at the second site | RPO | RTO | Cost | Fit |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | **Cold** | nothing; hardware racked, configuration as code (Appendix C.6), captures restored from archive | hours (last shipped archive) | hours (boot, restore, reconcile, re-certify sessions) | lowest | research tiers; firms whose venue has its own halt on site loss |
  | **Warm** | the replicas fold the shipped journal continuously (Ch. 111's replicated consumers across sites), sessions *not* connected, instruments and limits loaded | the shipping lag: ms–s | minutes (connect sessions, reconcile positions against drop-copy — Ch. 105 §105.4.5, pass the gate — Appendix M) | moderate | the common choice for order-path components |
  | **Hot** | a fully armed second instance with its own venue sessions, held *disarmed* or quoting a subset, fed by its own A/B lines | ~0 for market data (it has its own feed); the journal lag for order state | seconds (promote — Ch. 112's lease and fencing, now across sites; cancel-first — Ch. 107 §107.4.6) | highest (two of everything, two colocations) | market makers with resting quotes that must be pulled or inherited within seconds |

- **The first DR action is the kill, from the surviving site.** Whatever tier, the site that still has a venue session uses it — the purge port (Ch. 107 §107.4.5) held by the surviving site's supervisor sends the mass cancel — because the lost site's resting orders are live until cancel-on-disconnect fires (seconds, Ch. 106 §106.4.4) or someone cancels them. Split-brain across sites is the same problem as across hosts (Ch. 112): one lease, one fencing token, and a venue that enforces one session per token.
- **Venue-side DR.** Venues run their own secondary matching engines (a DR site with its own gateways and feeds, exercised on scheduled DR test days that firms are required to participate in); your A/B lines and sessions terminate at *their* primary, and the DR site's addresses, multicast groups and session parameters are a second configuration set (Appendix J.6's per-venue table, DR column) loaded by the readiness gate when the venue declares a failover — the sequence numbers and order state on the venue's DR side start from the venue's rules, not from yours (Ch. 106 §106.2.2's resync).
- **Drill it, measure it.** Site loss is a row in Ch. 116 §116.4.7's catalogue; the drill is a scheduled physical event (pull the cross-connect, drop the rack's power) with RTO and RPO measured from the ledger, and the result compared with the tier's objective; a venue's DR test day is the one opportunity to run the venue-side half against the real DR gateways.
